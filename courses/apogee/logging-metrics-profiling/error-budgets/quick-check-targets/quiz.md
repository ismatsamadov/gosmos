## Lantern Atoll promises 100%. How many passes may it lose in a quarter?

- [ ] One, to cover a single downpour
  > A target of 10000 leaves 10000 − 10000 = 0 basis points, so there is no pass to lose, not even one.
- [x] None, so the first downpour breaks the promise
  > The budget is `booked * 0 / 10000`, no passes at all, while the atoll's palms and downpours alone cost about one pass in a hundred.
- [ ] Eleven, one for each whole hundred passes booked
  > That is what 99%, the atoll's real target, allows; a promise of 100% allows no loss at all.

## At 99.5%, how many of Port Ellery's 1,104 passes in a quarter may it lose?

- [ ] 6, rounding 5.52 up to the nearest pass
  > A sixth loss leaves 1,098 of 1,104 flown, 99.46%, under the promised 99.5%: a budget never rounds up.
- [ ] 55, half a percent of 1,104 rounded off
  > Half a percent of 1,104 is 5.52, not 55.2; losing 55 passes would leave 95% flown.
- [x] 5, since the budget rounds down to whole passes
  > 1,104 × 50 / 10,000 is 5.52, and integer division keeps 5: losing 5 still leaves 99.55% flown.

## Salar Quieto ends the quarter with 3 of its 6 budget passes unspent. What are they?

- [x] Room for change that might cost a pass, such as recalibrating a dish
  > A budget is the room a promise leaves: what is left can go on a deploy or a recalibration without breaking the promise.
- [ ] A score to keep as high as possible, quarter after quarter
  > A budget kept whole every quarter only means nothing was changed that could have cost a pass; it is room, not a score.
- [ ] Passes owed to Salar Quieto's customers next quarter
  > A budget doesn't carry over or get paid back: each quarter's target counts that quarter's passes alone.
